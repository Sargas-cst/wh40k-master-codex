# Death Worlds & Recruiting Grounds

## The Classification {#classification}

A **Death World** — δτ-class in the Imperium's own notation — is a world classified by its
extremely dangerous or aggressive native flora and fauna, whose ecosystems are finely balanced
between continual destruction and lightning-fast reproduction. They take many forms, from
jungle-covered hell-holes with carnivorous plants and animals to barren volcanic wastelands
racked by ion storms.[^lex-death-world]

Humans can and do live on these worlds, but it is a never-ending struggle. On many of them **it
is as if the entire bio-mass of the planet were consciously motivated against human
settlement**, concentrating its forces against intruders to destroy them.[^lex-death-world]
Populations, where they exist, run from **1,000 to 15,000,000**.[^lex-death-world]

The lower bound is the striking figure. A world with a recorded population of a thousand is a
single settlement, and the Imperium keeps it in the ledger anyway. The reason is in the next
clause.

Death worlds are not usually inhabited by sapient species and are nearly impossible to colonise,
but they still need to be explored, which means outposts and support facilities. Some possess
rich mineral wealth, animal life, vegetation or gas deposits, so their tithe grades might range
from *Solutio Tertius* to *Solutio Prima*.[^lex-death-world] And inhabited death worlds can
provide **Deathworld Veteran Regiments** as part of that tithe.[^lex-death-world]

There is the mechanism this Chapter is about. A world too hostile to settle is still assigned a
tithe grade, and the tithe it pays is its people. The classification system of [[IV.T1.Ch2]]
does not distinguish between a planet that produces ore and a planet that produces survivors; both
are entries with a grade against them.

Two smaller entries in the definition are worth keeping. A world can be *made* into a death
world — on the Menagerie World Indiga, numerous captive giant predators escaped their zoos, and
when that happens the world is labelled a **"Man-Made Death World"**.[^lex-death-world] And some
Imperial ledgers refer to a death world as being *In Articulo Mortis* — "at the moment of
demise". Caliban was so described before its destruction.[^lex-death-world]

*In Articulo Mortis* is a clerk's phrase for a planet dying, and it was applied to the Dark
Angels' homeworld before that world was destroyed — which makes it, in retrospect, less a
classification than a forecast. Caliban is [[III.T3.Ch16]]'s.

The most famous and notorious death world is Catachan.[^lex-death-world]

## Catachan {#catachan}

Catachan lies in Ultima Segmentum and is covered in dense jungle, and **the planet's entire
ecosystem seems consciously hostile to all foreign life**. Every element of its native flora and
fauna represents a real danger to any human, and **even Space Marines are said to avoid this
hellish place**.[^lex-catachan]

The consequence is a piece of Imperial administration found nowhere else. **Catachan has no need
for a Planetary Defence Force**: its flora and fauna are sufficiently deadly to thwart attackers
without human intervention. The planet houses the single deadliest creature in the galaxy, and
some of its native wildlife is even released on *other* worlds to dissuade
invasion.[^lex-catachan]

Every other world in this Volume defends itself with walls, void shields, fleets or a garrison.
Catachan is defended by being Catachan. The Imperium's response was not to fix this but to
harvest it — exporting the wildlife as an area-denial weapon. Compare Cadia in [[II.T3.Ch8]],
which raised its entire population to hold a line; Catachan holds the line by existing, and the
population is surplus to that purpose. Which is why it is a tithe.

Its settlement history explains why anyone is there at all. Catachan has been colonised by humans
for longer than Imperial records can remember. When the first probes arrived, the planet was a
deceptive, calm green orb from orbit; when the colony ships crash-landed and the colonists had no
way to escape, they awoke to find themselves on one of the harshest planets in the galaxy,
barely surviving holed up in their spacecraft against a living, besieging
jungle.[^lex-catachan]

Nobody chose Catachan. A survey mistook it for habitable, the ships came down, and the ships could
not leave. Every Catachan alive descends from people who were trapped by a clerical error.

The settlements that can hold the jungle back are giant fortresses surrounded by vast cleared
plains that improve lines of fire — and holding them is a losing proposition. Building is difficult
because vines and lichen take hold anywhere, poisons melt the mortar, and vines can crush bunkers
and tanks. Buildings must constantly be rebuilt, and it seems that wherever they build, the jungle
redoubles its efforts to destroy them. Native wildlife gathers to repel the human intruders, and
eventually the numbers grow enough to **push the humans out of their settlement, so they must
resettle elsewhere**.[^lex-catachan]

The settlements are not permanent. They are positions held until the ecology evicts them, at
which point the population moves and starts again. Catachan is not a colonised world; it is a
world humans are being slowly expelled from, one fortress at a time, over a period longer than
Imperial records reach.

The threat inventory is worth stating because it explains what a Catachan is. **Every plant is
poisonous, making foraging impossible.** Some secrete poisonous pollen that destroys air-filters;
others secrete sticky liquid to capture passing animals and dissolve them; others poison the
ground to turn the surrounding area into boggy wasteland that traps intruders. In the jungle even
the slightest scratch can prove fatal as necrotic bacteria swarm to putrefy it. The **Catachan
Devil**, with jaws as big as a tank, is a major threat to human living centres, as are the giant
Shambling Mamorphs of the volcanic regions.[^lex-catachan] Even the climate is considered deadly
to all human life.[^lex-catachan]

Disease is a constant hazard: poxes and fevers that kill within hours, native insects carrying
virulent diseases on top of being venomous, so that a single bite is lethal in more ways than one.
Most of the more dangerous microbes cannot be seen at all, floating through the air until they
find a host. There are swamps where a single breath of the fog will burn a person's lungs out.
Even the water can prove fatal to drink. **Foot Rot** occurs after a few weeks in the jungle,
the rotten feet detaching from the legs. Many Catachans consider disease the greater
enemy.[^lex-catachan]

Under those conditions the culture is not a choice. Children are quick to learn survival tactics,
and only those who are fast and have a good natural aim survive to adulthood — **children often
learn to shoot before they walk**.[^lex-catachan] Set that beside Cadia, where children learned
the lasgun before they learned to read: on Cadia that was a curriculum, on Catachan it is
selection pressure. One world trains its children; the other loses the ones who cannot.

The people are recorded as hardy, pragmatic, with an easy grasp of realities and an uncomplicated
attitude towards danger[^lex-catachan] — and somewhat disdainful towards people from other worlds,
believing, with good reason, that few others in the galaxy have had an upbringing as dangerous as
their own.[^lex-catachan] They are physically and mentally resilient at a level normal humans
simply cannot match, and their heavy musculature has earned them the nickname "Baby
Ogryn" — though never to their faces, as it often results in serious
injury.[^lex-catachan] The Jungle Fighters are [[IV.T5.Ch17]]; the Ogryns proper,
[[IV.T2.Ch7]].

The economics are the point of the Section. Little production occurs in the few fortified
settlements, but Catachan is rich in resources it trades for essential goods — bio-specimens,
exotic vegetation, toxins and rare chemicals.[^lex-catachan] Military and medical supplies from
the Departmento Munitorum enable the settlements to survive and to continue raising a tithe of
soldiers, and **such is the combat prowess of Catachan's regiments that this arrangement has
remained unchanged for thousands of years**.[^lex-catachan] The Munitorum keeps a population alive
on an uninhabitable planet because the planet makes good infantry, and neither side has any
reason to change the terms.

Two recent episodes show how thin the connection is. During the Thirteenth Black Crusade, Catachan
lay in the path of both a wounded Void Whale and a Waaagh! led by the Freebooter Kaptain
Badrukk — and because of interference from the warp storm Bas Infernia, **the Imperium was unable
to warn the Catachans** of either.[^lex-catachan] Then, during the formation of the Great Rift,
Catachan was caught within it and exposed to daemonic invasion; when Guilliman's Indomitus Crusade
arrived, they found the local regiments **had already dealt with the
threat**.[^lex-catachan]

The Imperium could not get a message to Catachan, and did not need to. A relief force arrived to
find the daemons already killed by the planet's own troops. That is the most flattering thing this
codex can record about a death world, and also the most damning thing it can record about the
Imperium's care of one.

## Krieg {#krieg}

Krieg is the homeworld of the Death Korps of Krieg, notorious throughout the galaxy as grim and
fatalistic warriors, and **the planet is directly controlled by the Departmento Munitorum, who
gear it entirely towards war**.[^lex-krieg] It is classified a Death World and a **former Hive
World**,[^lex-krieg] and that second classification is the whole of its history.

Krieg's earliest history is unknown, shrouded in mystery and rumour; much of its historical record
has been lost to time or **deliberately purged**.[^lex-krieg] What is known is that Krieg was a
prosperous Hive World of billions in Segmentum Tempestus, specialising in manufacture and trade,
led by a council of Autocrats. As time passed the debauched and decadent Autocrats became paranoid
that an outside threat would take their power, and increasingly fortified the world against
outsiders — eventually coming to resent the Administratum's influence on a planet they considered
their own.[^lex-krieg]

In 433.M40 the High Autocrat declared independence from the Imperium and renounced the Emperor as
his divine master. Civil war broke out, and most of the planet quickly fell to the rebels except
for Hive Ferrograd, held by Colonel **Jurten** of the Krieg 83rd. Under strict orders not to let
Krieg fall, and knowing that no fleet on the scale needed to relieve him was available, Jurten
decided that **Krieg would either belong to the Emperor or to no one**. On the day of the Feast
of the Emperor's Ascension he unleashed a counter-attack of atomic cleansing — the
**Purging** — and for days Krieg was engulfed in nuclear fire.[^lex-krieg]

A loyalist officer, denied reinforcement, sterilised his own world rather than lose it. That is
the founding act of the Death Korps, and everything the Korps is derives from a decision one
colonel made about a planet of billions.

The aftermath is where the Imperium's part begins. The Departmento Munitorum took direct control
of Krieg, directed it entirely towards the perpetual war effort, and **raised its tithe grade to
the maximum**. Immediately after the conflict it demanded twenty full regiments from the
devastated world. Krieg paid them without hesitation, and the Munitorum was pleased to find how
well they proved themselves. Krieg's debt was not over: since the end of its shame it has
ceaselessly provided guardsmen and war material, and those unable to serve are consigned to work
in the industrial forges below the surface.[^lex-krieg]

Note the sequence. A planet destroys itself to remain loyal, and the Imperium's response is to
raise its tax. The word the sources use for the obligation is **debt**, and the debt is owed by
the loyalists' descendants for a rebellion the loyalists put down. Compare Armageddon in
[[II.T3.Ch8]], whose surviving defenders were sterilised and sent to work camps: the Imperium's
treatment of populations that win its battles is consistent, and it is not gratitude.

What Krieg is now follows from the Purging. It remains a barren death world wracked by radiation
storms and uninhabitable toxicity levels, and **much of its population growth is undertaken by
the Vitae Womb system**.[^lex-krieg] With the surface an uninhabitable ruin, the population lives
entirely underground in subterranean cities supporting forges that churn out war
material.[^lex-krieg]

Life there is exceptionally grim even by the standards of the Imperium. The population subsists
only on nutrient slime and vat-grown gruel, carefully rationed and in short supply. Exhaustion,
malnutrition and industrial accidents keep **civilian life expectancy only scarcely higher than
that of a Krieg soldier at the front**.[^lex-krieg] Yet such is the stoic, repentant and
self-sacrificial nature of the people that few complain: most toil with religious fervour, and any
who show a hint of discontent are viewed as social pariahs. This has made Krieg **a place where
rebellion is all but unheard of**.[^lex-krieg]

Read those two sentences together. Krieg is the only world in this Volume where being a civilian
is as lethal as being a soldier, and it is also the world with no dissent. The mechanism is
recorded plainly: the people of Krieg have felt an eternal shame ever since the civil war, and it
manifests as stoicism and an acceptance of expending their own lives to regain their honour in
the Emperor's eyes.[^lex-krieg] All citizens are expected to further the Imperium's endless wars,
with even the infirm, wounded or too weak to fight contributing through production. Those capable
of fighting are drilled from early childhood and heavily indoctrinated so that **their identity is
subsumed by this culture**.[^lex-krieg]

Inherited guilt, industrially maintained, is a more efficient instrument of control than
Cadia's martial law or Vigilus's water castes. Krieg needs no secret police because the population
is policing itself for a crime committed in 433.M40 by people they are not descended from — their
ancestors were on the other side.

One further detail deserves recording, because the Imperium is unable to explain it. **Krieg
produces next to no psykers**, and whether this is due to conditions on the planet or to
systematic culling of the "genelost" remains unknown.[^lex-krieg] A world with no psykers is
anomalous in a galaxy where the human psychic population is described as rising — [[I.T2.Ch6]] holds
the scale — and the sources offer two explanations without choosing: an environmental effect, or an
undocumented programme of murder.

Then there are the names, which are the most complete expression of the culture. Names are rarely
used on Krieg; Kriegsmen are usually referred to only by an identifying
number.[^lex-krieg] A Kriegsman can obtain — or re-earn — a name in a few ways: by promotion to
officer, by being martyred, or by an act of bravery.[^lex-krieg] The Krieg believe **these names
are how the Emperor will recognise them upon their death**, and it is believed by outsiders,
though not certain, that they choose their names to match one of Jurten's loyalists. To have a
name is an honour that almost no award or commendation could equal.[^lex-krieg]

A soldier who is a number until he dies well enough to be given a name, and who chooses the name
of one of the men who burned his planet. The Death Korps is [[IV.T5.Ch17]]; the Emperor they
expect to be recognised by is [[III.T3.Ch17]]'s.

## What the Imperium Takes From Them {#tithe}

Set the two worlds side by side and the pattern of this Title completes itself.

Catachan pays a tithe of soldiers because its environment produces people who can fight, and it
receives military and medical supplies in return — an arrangement unchanged for thousands of
years.[^lex-catachan] Krieg pays a tithe at the maximum grade because its population believes it
owes one, and its planetary government *is* the Departmento
Munitorum.[^lex-krieg] Catachan is a trade; Krieg is a debt. Both deliver infantry, and the
Imperium's ledger records them the same way.

The mechanism behind both is a single line in the classification: inhabited death worlds can
provide Deathworld Veteran Regiments as part of their tithe.[^lex-death-world] That is a policy,
not an accident. The Imperium noticed that worlds which kill most of their inhabitants produce
excellent soldiers from the remainder, and wrote the observation into its tax code.

Two consequences follow, and they are the reason death worlds belong in a Volume about geography
rather than only in one about armies.

The first is that these worlds are not governed in any ordinary sense. Catachan has no PDF because
it does not need one.[^lex-catachan] Krieg has no local government because the Munitorum
administers it directly.[^lex-krieg] Cadia had no Arbites.[^lex-cadia] The further a
world moves towards being purely a military resource, the fewer of the Imperium's civil
institutions it retains — until, at the limit, the tithe office is the only Imperial body present.

The second is that a death world's population is the only thing on it the Imperium values, and
the Imperium does not value it much. Catachan received no warning of two incoming invasions
because a warp storm was in the way.[^lex-catachan] Krieg's civilians die at roughly the rate of
its front-line troops.[^lex-krieg] Terra's population lives in squalor hoping a child might
become a clerk, as [[II.T2.Ch4]] recorded, and Ultramar — the best-governed realm in the
galaxy — gets its people to their mid-thirties, as [[II.T2.Ch6]] recorded. Against that scale
Catachan and Krieg are not aberrations. They are the same policy with the padding removed.

What the classification actually describes, then, is not a kind of planet. It is a kind of
relationship: worlds the Imperium cannot hold, does not administer, and will not defend, which it
keeps on the books because they produce the one commodity it never has enough of. The regiments
they produce are [[IV.T5.Ch16]] and [[IV.T5.Ch17]]; the tithe that takes them, [[IV.T1.Ch2]].
