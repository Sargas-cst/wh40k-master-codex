# Traitor's Pact & Bio-Transference

## The Offer {#the-offer}

The Necrontyr had lost. Exiled among the Halo Stars, their unity spent and their hatred
generalised into loathing of all life, they had no route back — and then something offered
them one.

**Biotransference** was the process by which the organic Necrontyr race were transformed
into the mechanical immortal Necrons.[^lex-biotransference] The circumstances are recorded
precisely. The Necrons were facing total defeat when Szarekh, the Silent King, was
approached by one of the **C'tan** — the one known to history as the **Deceiver**. This
C'tan promised immortality and power **at the expense of their organic
forms**.[^lex-biotransference]

Notice that the terms were disclosed. The Deceiver did not conceal the price; it named it.
What the Necrontyr were told they would lose was their bodies, and what they were not
told — the part that made the name stick — was what else went with them.

The decision was not impulsive. **After months of debate**, the Triarchs approved the
C'tan's plan to shed their organic bodies for immortal mechanical forms known as
Necrons.[^lex-biotransference]

Months of debate is a detail worth holding onto. This was not a species tricked in a
moment of panic. It was a deliberative body that examined an offer at length and accepted
it. Whatever the Deceiver's name implies, the Necrontyr were participants.

The alliance the pact created is described from the other side too. The Necrontyr forged
their alliance with the vampiric C'tan — star gods of immense power, given **living metal
forms of the Necrontyr's own half-forgotten pantheon** by the first Necrontyr who found
them.[^lex-war-in-heaven-necron] The Deceiver said that its race too had fought and lost a
war with the Old Ones; whether that was true is unknown, but the consequences of the
alliance are clear.[^lex-war-in-heaven-necron]

Two things follow. First, the gods the Necrontyr swore themselves to were wearing shapes
the Necrontyr had made — the Solar Gods of [[III.T1.Ch1]]'s pantheon, given metal bodies.
A dying astrological culture met something in space and dressed it as the gods it already
had. What the C'tan actually are is [[I.T4.Ch14]]'s subject.

Second, the Deceiver's claim to have fought the Old Ones and lost is the oldest recorded
piece of propaganda in the setting, and the sources decline to verify it. It made the
C'tan a fellow victim rather than a patron, which is precisely what a species too proud to
accept charity needed to hear.

Not everyone agreed. **Not all of the Necron Dynasties willingly went along with the
plan** — some, including Trazyn the Infinite, Orikan the Diviner and the Khansu Dynasty,
**had to be forced**.[^lex-biotransference]

So the transformation was, in part, done to its subjects against their will by their own
government. The two named holdouts are among the most consequential Necrons in the
setting, and both of them were conscripted into immortality.

## The Biofurnaces {#biofurnaces}

What the process physically involved is recorded in one of the most concrete passages in
the setting's deep history, and it should be read slowly.

Under the oversight of **Szeras**, colossal biofurnaces roared day and night, **consuming
bodies of weak flesh and replacing them with enduring forms of living
metal**.[^lex-biotransference] Base Necrontyr children were converted into Necron
Warriors.[^lex-biotransference]

An entire civilisation fed itself into industrial machinery, by dynasty, including its
children. The scale is what distinguishes this from every other transformation in the
setting: the Space Marine implants of [[IV.T4.Ch12]] are applied to volunteers one at a
time, and biotransference was applied to a species as a manufacturing process.

And while the machines worked, so did the C'tan. **The C'tan swarmed about the
biotransference sites, drinking in the torrent of cast-off life energy and growing ever
stronger.**[^lex-biotransference]

There is the part of the bargain that was not disclosed. The souls the Necrontyr shed had
to go somewhere, and the beings conducting the procedure were feeding on them as they came
off. The C'tan did not merely receive an army; they were paid in the discarded species.
Compare the accounts in [[I.T1.Ch3]] of what the Warp does with a soul at death — these
souls never reached it.

## What Came Out {#what-came-out}

The Necrons that emerged were **stronger**, and **devoid of free will, bound to their
masters through command protocols**.[^lex-biotransference]

The distribution of what survived is the cruellest detail in the Chapter, because it
tracks the class structure of [[III.T1.Ch1]] exactly. **Few Necrons retained independent
personalities or sentience. Only the high-ranking nobles had been given the extensive
circuitry to retain their previous personalities.**[^lex-biotransference]

Selfhood was an expensive component, and it was fitted according to rank. The nobility
went into the furnaces and came out as themselves; the commoners came out as machinery
that walks. A feudal society converted its own hierarchy into hardware, and the result is
permanent in a way no social order made of flesh can be — there is no generation coming
that might be born equal.

Others were shaped for function rather than preserved. Triarch Praetorians and Lychguards
were **programmed with new loyalties and instincts to fit their intended
role**.[^lex-biotransference] And one suggestion in the sources is worth recording as the
suggestion it is: it has been proposed that the destructive madness of Necron Destroyers
**may be a subconscious imperative driven into them during
biotransference**.[^lex-biotransference]

Loyalty as a programmed instinct is the logical end of the honour-and-oath culture the
Necrontyr already had. They did not invent a new form of obedience; they made the existing
one non-optional. The Necrons as they now exist are [[VI.T1.Ch1]].

## The Second Conflict {#second-conflict}

The pact worked. That is the uncomfortable fact underneath everything else in this
Chapter.

Under the command of their C'tan masters, the Necrontyr were able to **breach the
Webway** — and turned the tide against the Old Ones, forcing their retreat, though not
their destruction.[^lex-war-in-heaven-necron]

The Webway had been the thing they could not beat. [[III.T1.Ch1]] closed with the
Necrontyr exiled precisely because the Old Ones could move and they could not; the C'tan
solved that, and the war reversed. The Old Ones' great work became an attack route.

What followed was not a campaign so much as a harvest. The new Necrons and their C'tan
**extinguished suns and consumed entire solar systems**, besieging the fortresses of the
Old Ones and **harvesting the races the Old Ones nurtured like cattle to feed the
C'tan**.[^lex-war-in-heaven-necron]

The Old Ones' founding belief — that all life was useful — met a power that agreed with it
and meant something else by it. Every species they had seeded became feedstock.

And then the victors got bored, which is the detail that tells you what the C'tan are.
Defying conventional strategy, so assured of their conquest of the universe, **the C'tan
playfully unleashed battles against each other while building and smashing countless of
their own cities** — until the cattle grew too few, at which point **the C'tan began
cannibalising one another**.[^lex-war-in-heaven-necron]

A war they had already won became a game, and when the food ran out the players ate each
other. The C'tan reduced their own number without any enemy's help, and that
self-inflicted attrition is what eventually makes [[III.T1.Ch5]] possible. The Necrons did
not defeat gods at full strength. They waited.

## The Long Regret {#regret}

The pact's reckoning arrives in a single sentence, and it is the moment the Silent King
becomes the figure the rest of Necron history turns on.

**As Szarekh watched the C'tan feast on the energy of his people, he finally realised he
had been betrayed.** Though now immortal and eventually victorious over the Old Ones, the
Necrons **had effectively traded their souls**.[^lex-biotransference]

He did nothing about it for a long time. Szarekh bided his time until the Old Ones were
destroyed before striking.[^lex-biotransference] That restraint is either statesmanship or
complicity depending on how one reads it, and the sources do not tell the reader which:
the Silent King learned he had sold his species and then finished the war anyway. The
revolt is [[III.T1.Ch5]].

The regret did not end with him, and it has become policy. In the modern era **many Necron
Dynasties resent biotransference and see it as a mistake**, and seek out new races that
could provide suitable host bodies for organic forms. Some, like the Nephrekh Dynasty,
have **reversing biotransference as their primary
motivation**.[^lex-biotransference]

That reframes the Necrons entirely as an antagonist. A faction whose central project is
undoing what was done to it is not conquering the galaxy for territory; it is looking for
bodies. The species that came to hate all life is now shopping among the living.

The last detail is the quietest and the worst. **Most Necrons have few memories of the
time before biotransference — only vague flashes of events, in the way one might remember
early youth.**[^lex-biotransference]

They cannot properly remember being alive. Sixty million years of existence rests on a
grievance about a loss its holders can no longer picture, sustained by nobles who kept
their personalities and enforced on legions who did not. Whatever the Necrontyr were is
gone in the only sense that matters: the beings who paid for immortality can no longer
recall what they paid with.
