# The Silent King's Rebellion & Sleep

## The Office of the Silent King {#the-office}

The revolt that ends the War in Heaven was carried out by one office, and the office is
worth understanding before the act, because its design explains both why the revolt
succeeded and why it could only be attempted once.

The **Silent King** is the title of the historic ruler of the Necrontyr.[^lex-silent-king]
He was the most pre-eminent member of the **Triarch** and **by tradition never spoke**,
instead issuing edicts to the two other Phaerons on the council. The King and his two
Triarchs issued the edicts that bound the various Dynasties, and **the ruler's command was
law, against which disobedience meant death**.[^lex-silent-king] A Royal Court of lesser
nobles, advisors, astrologers and physicians surrounded them.[^lex-silent-king]

Two details of that arrangement matter. The astrologers and physicians are the Necrontyr
civilisation of [[III.T1.Ch1]] in miniature — a court staffed to read the stars and to
inspect the ruler for tumours. And the silence is not affectation but procedure: the
supreme authority of the Necrontyr communicated only through two intermediaries, which
means every edict in their history reached the Dynasties at second hand.

The succession was nominally hereditary, **though the uncertain lifespans of the Necrontyr
ensured the title of Silent King had passed from one dynasty to another many
times**.[^lex-silent-king]

There is the radiation curse showing up in constitutional law. A hereditary office held by
a species that dies young and unpredictably is hereditary in name only; in practice the
throne circulated among the dynasties because its holders kept dying. That instability is
the same pressure that produced the Wars of Secession, and it is why the Triarch of
[[III.T1.Ch1]] needed an external enemy in the first place.

Only one Silent King is named: **Szarekh, the last**.[^lex-silent-king] Everything that
follows in this Chapter is his.

## The Revolt {#revolt}

Szarekh had known he was betrayed since he watched the C'tan feed at the biofurnaces, as
[[III.T1.Ch2]] records. What he did with that knowledge was wait.

**Throughout the final stages of the War in Heaven, Szarekh bided his time, waiting for the
moment when the C'tan would be most vulnerable.** With the Old Ones finally defeated, the
Silent King struck and led a Necron revolt against the arrogant
C'tan.[^lex-necron]

The timing is the whole of the strategy, and it is colder than any battle in this Volume.
He finished the war for his masters first. Every campaign the Necrons fought after Szarekh
knew the truth was fought to bring the C'tan closer to the exhaustion that would make them
killable. The Old Ones were exterminated by an ally who had already decided to turn.

The conditions he waited for are recorded from the other side. The C'tan were **tired and
hungry from the war they waged** and therefore vulnerable — and Szarekh, **the only
surviving member of the Triarch**, saw an opportunity to free his
people.[^lex-war-in-heaven-necron] They had also spent themselves: [[III.T1.Ch2]] describes
the C'tan cannibalising one another once the harvest ran short. Szarekh did not defeat gods
at their height. He struck a diminished pantheon that had eaten much of itself.

The weapon was the war's own accumulated knowledge. **The Necrons focused the unimaginable
energies of the living universe into weapons too mighty for even the C'tan to
endure.**[^lex-necron] Their technological abilities had accelerated enormously across the
long war with C'tan aid — and at the end, as Necrons, they were wielding the very energies
of the universe, and turned that arcane weaponry on the star gods
themselves.[^lex-necrontyr]

The C'tan had made their servants capable of killing them. That is the second betrayal in
the pact and it runs the other way: the Deceiver's gift of power was not conditional, and
sixty million years of Necron technology is entirely inherited from what the C'tan taught
during the war.

The outcome was not death, because death was not available. **The C'tan, almost impossible
to destroy entirely due to their very nature, were instead shattered into
shards.**[^lex-necron] In a moment of arrogance they were unprepared for an unrelenting
rebellion among their own forces, and the Necrons sundered them into **thousands of shards**
and imprisoned those within **Tesseract Labyrinths**.[^lex-war-in-heaven-necron] Their final
defeat came at the **Battle of the Sokar Gate**.[^lex-war-in-heaven-necron] Millions of
Necrons were destroyed in the rebellion, but the Necrons were finally
free.[^lex-war-in-heaven-necron]

Shattered rather than killed, and then kept. The C'tan shards of [[I.T4.Ch15]] are not
survivors who escaped; they are the fragments of gods held as equipment by the species that
broke them. The Necrons went to war against their own deities, won, and then filed the
pieces.

## The Great Sleep {#great-sleep}

Victory left the Necrons in a position their King read accurately and without
self-flattery.

Even with both the Old Ones and the C'tan defeated, **the Silent King saw that the time of
the Necrons was — for the moment — over**. The mantle of galactic domination would soon pass
to the Eldar, who had fought alongside the Old Ones; the Necrons, weakened by the war and
by the revolt, **could not stand against them**.[^lex-necron]

That is a remarkable assessment for the victor of a sixty-million-year war to make about
itself, and it is the reason the Necrons still exist. Having destroyed the galaxy's first
civilisation and its own gods, the Necron leadership concluded it would lose to the
survivors, and acted on that conclusion rather than on its record.

The reasoning that followed is the coldest sentence in the Title: **the Silent King knew
that the time of the Eldar would pass, as did the time of all flesh.**[^lex-necron]

The Necrons did not retreat from the Aeldari. They outwaited them. A species that has
already abandoned mortality can treat a rival civilisation as a phase, and the entire
strategy rests on the one advantage biotransference actually delivered — that the Necrons
have no deadline.

So the remaining Necron cities were transformed into great tomb complexes threaded with
**stasis-crypts**, and the Necrons were laid to rest, **ordered to sleep for sixty million
years and then reawaken, ready to rebuild all that was lost and restore the dynasties to
their former glory**.[^lex-necron] Those complexes are the Tomb Worlds of [[II.T3.Ch10]],
and the number in that order is why the Imperium keeps finding them: the sleep was not
indefinite. It had a set term, and the term has expired.

Szarekh did not join them. **Destroying the command protocols by which he had controlled
his people, the Silent King left the galaxy, there to find whatever measure of solace or
penance he could.**[^lex-necron]

Read that against [[III.T1.Ch2]]. The command protocols were the instrument of the
Necrons' enslavement — the circuitry through which the C'tan and then Szarekh held a species
without free will. His last act as ruler was to destroy the mechanism that made him ruler,
and then to exile himself. The word the sources use is **penance**, and it is the only
instance in this Volume of a leader accepting that a decision was his fault. He had
approved biotransference after months of debate; he freed what was left of his people from
his own control and left.

## What the Sleep Cost {#attrition}

The Great Sleep is usually described as a strategy. It was also a slow disaster, and the
sources are specific about it.

**For sixty million years the Necrons remained in their deathless slumber**. As time
passed, **many Tomb Worlds fell prey to malfunction or ill-fortune. Some were destroyed by
marauding Eldar. These failures destroyed millions, if not billions, of dormant
Necrons.**[^lex-necron]

Billions, lost to equipment failure and to raids on the sleeping. The plan that treated
flesh as a phase to be outwaited had a defect its author did not price: sixty million years
is long enough for the machinery to break. Set that beside [[II.T3.Ch10]], where a Deep
Spirit runs each Tomb World and Canoptek constructs maintain it — that maintenance
apparatus exists because the alternative was demonstrated at scale.

And the Aeldari did not simply inherit the galaxy; some of them spent the interval hunting.
The species Szarekh judged too strong to fight went looking for the tombs. Which makes the
Aeldari–Necron enmity of the 41st Millennium considerably older than either side's current
grievance.

The galaxy the sleep left behind is the starting condition for the rest of this Volume.
**Through some unknown means the Aeldari survived the Enslavers**, and inherited the mantle
of galactic dominion in the war's wake, founding the Eldar Empire; weakened by the revolt
and the aeons of war before it, Szarekh led the Necrons into their slumber beneath the Tomb
Worlds.[^lex-war-in-heaven-necron]

"Through some unknown means" is the sources declining to explain the single most consequential
survival in galactic history. The species that inherited everything did so for reasons no
record gives, and this codex reports the gap rather than filling it.

What is left at the end of the War in Heaven is worth stating as a ledger, because the whole
of the Imperium's history happens inside it. The galaxy's first civilisation:
exterminated. Its gods: shattered and imprisoned by their own servants. Its greatest
predator: still extant, and merely starved back. Its victors: asleep under the ground on
worlds nobody has counted, with an alarm set. And its inheritors: the Aeldari, holding a
galaxy emptied by a plague, with no explanation of why they lived.

Everything from [[III.T2.Ch6]] onward — the Dark Age of Technology, the Age of Strife, the
Emperor, the Imperium — takes place in the ruins of this, on worlds seeded by the losers,
above tombs whose occupants set an alarm for a date that has now passed. The Aeldari Empire
that inherited it all fell too, and [[s:fall-of-the-eldar|that fall]] is treated with the survivors
who carry it. Humanity's rise happens in [[III.T2.Ch6]], and nothing in it is aware of any of this.
