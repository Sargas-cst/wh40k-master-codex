# Prehistoric Powers (Old Ones vs Necrontyr)

## The First Sentient Life {#old-ones}

Everything in this Volume descends from a war fought before humanity existed, and to
understand that war you have to begin with the fact that one side had already won
everything.

The **Old Ones** were an ancient, space-faring, reptilian race with an advanced
civilisation long before the development of the Young Races. They were **the first of all
the galaxy's sentient life**, and the first race to cross the sea of stars — the oldest
space-faring species there has ever been.[^lex-old-ones]

Their character, as the sources describe it, is not martial. They are said to have had a
slow, cold-blooded wisdom, studying the stars and raising astrology and astronomy to an
arcane science; it was their understanding of the universe that let them manipulate
alternate dimensions, and they are known to have undertaken great works of psychic
engineering.[^lex-old-ones] At some unknown point they crafted the **Webway**, a conduit
through which they could travel to far-flung worlds without suffering the tides of the
Warp.[^lex-old-ones] Such was their science that they could cross vast tracts of space
in a single step by way of Webway portals, and by such means they spread their spawn to
many other places.[^lex-old-ones]

Note what that gives them, because it decides the war. The Webway — the subject of
[[I.T3.Ch10]] — is not a weapon. It is infrastructure, built to avoid the Warp rather
than to fight with it, and it grants instantaneous movement across the galaxy. A power
that can appear anywhere without crossing the space in between does not need a fleet.

Their governing belief is stated plainly, and it is the reason the galaxy is populated at
all: **the Old Ones believed that all life was useful**. They are known to have brought
about the rise of numerous new species and to have impregnated thousands of worlds which
they made their own.[^lex-old-ones]

The Old Slann held that the object of life was to perfect their mortal souls, having
learned that a sufficiently powerful mind would remain whole within the Warp after death
and could reincarnate as it wished — **provided it could resist the diseased
consciousnesses of the Powers of Chaos, which could consume its
identity**.[^lex-old-ones]

That last clause is the most important sentence in this Chapter, and it is easy to read
past. Sixty-five million years before the 41st Millennium, the galaxy's first
civilisation already knew that the Warp held predatory intelligences capable of devouring
a soul, and had built its entire spiritual practice around resisting them. The Chaos of
[[V.T1.Ch1]] is not a late development in this setting. It was there before anyone, and
the Old Ones understood it well enough to plan around it — which makes what happens in
[[III.T1.Ch4]] a failure of caution rather than of knowledge.

## A Race Born Dying {#necrontyr}

The other side of the war begins not with ambition but with a diagnosis.

The **Necrontyr** were an ancient race of humanoids, now long extinct.[^lex-necrontyr]
Their planet of origin was barren and radiation-blasted, incredibly hostile to life.
Their bodies were ridden with radiation sickness and they lived a morbid life, constantly
fearful of their coming deaths. **Their cities were built in anticipation of their demise
— little more than vast tomb complexes with a few temporary homes for the
living.**[^lex-necrontyr]

Read that as architecture and it tells you everything about the culture. A Necrontyr city
was a cemetery with accommodation attached. The permanent structures were for the dead;
the living were housed provisionally, because they were provisional.

They tried to fix it. The Necrontyr attempted to overcome their mortality by scientific
means, and realised after thousands of years that their bodies simply could not be
changed to survive on that planet.[^lex-necrontyr] So they left instead — developing
spacefaring technology, though of a much slower and more uncertain nature than the Old
Ones'. Where the Old Ones moved between stars in seconds, the Necrontyr used slow ships
fitted with **stasis crypts** to reach out to the stars, clad in living metal to withstand
the rigours of flight.[^lex-necrontyr] And it did not work either: despite establishing
interstellar colonies, they still could not escape their genetic curse, and continued to
die quickly of tumorous growths.[^lex-necrontyr]

The detail that conveys the daily texture of this is a ritual. On waking each morning, a
Necrontyr performed the **Rite of Expiscation** — checking the body for any sign of the
cancerous growths that heralded imminent death.[^lex-necrontyr] An entire sect of priests,
the **Onomancers**, was developed to treat the affliction, to no
avail.[^lex-necrontyr] Mortuary cults and funeral rituals including embalming and
mummification were prominent, and society as a whole was dominated by
death.[^lex-necrontyr]

A civilisation that begins every day by inspecting itself for tumours is not going to
produce a hopeful philosophy. Set it against the Old Slann's belief that the point of
life was to perfect the soul: one species treated mortality as a problem of spiritual
craft, the other as a medical emergency that never ended.

Their politics were rigid and unstable at once. Like the Necrons who followed, the
Necrontyr were organised into a feudal dynastic system pledging fealty to the
**Triarch**, led by the **Silent King**, and defined by a rigid system of honour, oaths
and obedience to one's liege. Society divided into three classes: the nobility and
priests, the soldiery, and the commoners.[^lex-necrontyr] It was highly ritualistic, and
even wars were said to be dominated by formality and procedure.[^lex-necrontyr] Yet
inter-dynastic conflict and civil strife were a constant concern, as reflected in the
**Wars of Secession**.[^lex-necrontyr]

Two other details matter for what comes later. Before they encountered the C'tan, the
Necrontyr had a pantheon of **Solar Gods**.[^lex-necrontyr] And they placed particular
emphasis on astrology, divination and the zodiac constellations visible from their
homeworld — constellations their priests expressed in hand symbols, which Crypteks would
later use to channel their techno-arcane abilities.[^lex-necrontyr]

So both civilisations were astronomers and both were mystics. The difference is that the
Old Ones' star-watching produced dimensional engineering, and the Necrontyr's produced a
religion about the sun. When something answered that religion, in [[III.T1.Ch2]], they
recognised it.

Technologically they were formidable but outclassed. Before the C'tan, the Necrontyr were
a highly advanced civilisation capable of establishing an interstellar empire and were
masters of material technology — though not able to match the Old Ones — and were already
using living metal.[^lex-necrontyr] The distinction in that sentence is precise and worth
keeping: **masters of *material* technology.** The Old Ones manipulated alternate
dimensions. The Necrontyr built extremely good machines. It is the difference between the
Materium and the Immaterium set out in [[I.T1.Ch3]], expressed as a gap between two
civilisations' capabilities.

## The Meeting {#the-meeting}

What happened when the two races met is recorded not as a diplomatic event but as an
emotional one, and the sources are unusually direct about it.

As the Old Ones passed through the cosmos, younger and fiercer races developed in their
wake, the Necrontyr among them — struggling in their colonisation of other worlds. During
that encounter the Old Ones were seen to have colonised other worlds with far greater
ease, and to possess an immense longevity approaching immortality.[^lex-old-ones]

Then the sentence that starts the war: **this meeting between a short-lived, dour race
and a hopeful, nigh-immortal one set a fire of rage burning in the hearts of the
Necrontyr. From that moment on, they turned their entire lives to destroying the Old
Ones.**[^lex-necrontyr]

Almost immediately the Necrontyr resented these long-lived beings and the magnificent
works they had created.[^lex-war-in-heaven-necron] What tipped resentment into war was a
refusal: the Old Ones were unwilling to share the secrets of their seemingly immortal
lifespans.[^lex-war-in-heaven-necron]

That is the causal core of the whole Volume, and it deserves stating plainly. The oldest
war in the setting was started by a dying species that met a species which did not die,
asked how, and was told no.

But there is a second cause recorded alongside it, and it is colder. The Triarch was
desperate to restore a measure of unity their race had known before the Wars of Secession,
and **realised that unity could come from an external threat**. Seeing the Old Ones as a
suitable candidate for that threat, the Triarchs struck.[^lex-war-in-heaven-necron]

So the war was also a domestic policy. A ruling council facing secessionist rebellions
picked a foreign enemy to reunify the population, and chose the most powerful civilisation
in the galaxy for the purpose. The Imperium of [[IV.T1.Ch1]] governs by the same
instrument, and it is worth noticing that the setting's first recorded act of statecraft
is this one.

The sources do not reconcile the two accounts, and they do not need to: grief and calculation
are not exclusive. The Necrontyr hated the Old Ones for living, and the Triarch found that
hatred useful.

## The First Conflict {#first-conflict}

The first phase of the war is a story about logistics, and the Necrontyr lost it.

They struck, abandoning the rebellions, and plunged their race into a devastating
war.[^lex-war-in-heaven-necron] Despite superior numbers and advanced technologies, after
aeons of warfare the Necrontyr realised they could not hope to defeat the Old Ones **or
the superior tactics their Webway afforded**, and were pushed to the edge of the galaxy
amidst the **Halo Stars**, where they would be little more than a minor annoyance to the
seemingly unstoppable Old Ones.[^lex-war-in-heaven-necron]

The Necrontyr account of the same defeat is bleaker: they were **constantly outmanoeuvred**
by the Old Ones' mastery of the Webway, and pushed back to being merely an annoyance in
the outer regions.[^lex-necrontyr]

Superior numbers and better material technology lost to a transport network. That is the
first demonstration of a rule this setting never stops proving: in a galaxy this size,
the power that controls movement controls the war. It is why the Astronomican matters so
much in [[II.T1.Ch1]], and why the Cadian Gate mattered in [[II.T3.Ch8]].

Defeat then did what defeat does to a coalition held together by an external threat. As
the prospect of victory faded, so did the unity that had been its whole purpose; the
Necrontyr descended into disunity and secessionist war once more, **allowing the Old Ones
to return to their quest of engineering and uplifting the life in the
galaxy**.[^lex-war-in-heaven-necron]

The Triarch's plan failed on its own terms. It picked a war to end the rebellions, lost
the war, and got the rebellions back.

What the Necrontyr became during that long confinement is the hinge of the Title. Their
fury cooled after many thousands of years of imprisonment among the Halo Stars — and
**their disgust of the Old Ones turned into an utter hatred of all
life**.[^lex-necrontyr]

That is the transformation that matters more than any battle. They began by hating one
species for being immortal. They emerged hating existence itself. Everything the Necrons
do for the next sixty million years — the harvesting, the sterilised worlds, the Pariah
Nexus — follows from a grudge that generalised while its holders sat at the edge of the
galaxy with nothing to do but resent. The Necrons are [[VI.T1.Ch1]]; what they became is
[[III.T1.Ch2]].

    The War in Heaven was fought roughly **sixty-five million** years before the 41st
    Millennium, or roughly **sixty million**.[^lex-war-in-heaven-necron]

    The article gives both in a single clause — "roughly sixty-five to sixty million years
    before" — with separate citations, so the range is a span between two published figures
    rather than one source's estimate of a long war. Since nothing in the setting dates the
    war's phases individually, the codex uses the range and avoids implying a start or an
    end.

The Old Ones, meanwhile, went back to work. That is the state of the galaxy at the end of
this Chapter: one civilisation seeding life across thousands of worlds, and one dying
civilisation exiled at the rim, no longer fighting, and no longer capable of being
reconciled to anything. What changed it was not a weapon. It was a meeting, and it is the
subject of the next Chapter.
