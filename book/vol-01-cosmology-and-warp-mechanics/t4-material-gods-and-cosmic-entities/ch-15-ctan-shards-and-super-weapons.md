# C'tan Shards & Super-Weapons

## Anatomy of a Shard {#shard}

The C'tan were not defeated. They were dismantled by their own worshippers, and the pieces
kept.

After the C'tan had led the Necrons through the war against the Old Ones, their followers
turned on them and shattered most of them into shards — to be used as weapons of
war.[^lex-c-tan] That is the whole of the transaction, stated plainly, and it is one of the
few unambiguous victories anyone in this setting has won against a god. The rebellion itself
belongs to [[III.T1.Ch5]].

What remains are entities of near-unlimited power. A shard can project blasts of energy,
control the minds of lesser beings, manipulate the flow of time, and banish its enemies into
alternate realities.[^lex-c-tan-shard] The phrase "near-unlimited" is doing a great deal of
work, and it is worth noticing that this is a *fragment* — the reduced remainder of
something the sources decline to bound at all.

Two things follow from the shattering, and they pull in opposite directions.

The first is that the Necrons acquired a weapons stockpile of enormous power. A shard is
biddable in a way a whole C'tan never was; the point of breaking them was to make them
usable, and it worked.

The second is that they multiplied their problem. Where there were a handful of star gods,
there are now many shards, each retaining the appetites of the original. Nothing in the
material suggests a shard has been reconciled to its condition or has forgotten what it was.
The Necrons hold their gods in pieces, and the arrangement is stable only for as long as the
containment holds — which is why the devices described below exist, and why so much Necron
engineering is addressed to keeping its own arsenal shut.

Set this against [[I.T4.Ch14]]: destroying a C'tan's necrodermis achieves nothing permanent,
because the essence simply returns. Shattering, by contrast, is permanent. The difference
between a setback and a dismemberment is the difference between attacking the vessel and
attacking the thing inside it, and only the Necrons ever worked out how to do the second.

## Tesseract Labyrinths and Vaults {#tesseract}

Holding a being of pure energy requires a box that works on energy, and the Necrons built
two sizes of it.

A **tesseract labyrinth** is a fist-sized cube capable of imprisoning any being — including
those of pure energy, such as daemons — which is exactly why daemonhunters prize
it.[^lex-tesseract-labyrinth] Its operation is described with almost comic simplicity: a
push-button interface engages the device's zero-point reactor, which imprisons the target
within its chambers.[^lex-tesseract-labyrinth]

The contrast with the rest of the setting is the point. The Imperium's answer to a daemon is
the Ordo Malleus, the Grey Knights, and a theology of sanctified war, treated at
[[IV.T7.Ch25]]. The Necron answer is a cube with a button. Whether that is a measure of
Necron sophistication or of Imperial futility is left for the reader; both readings are
available and neither is flattering.

A **Tesseract Vault** is the same principle at siege scale: a large Necron war engine built
around a captured C'tan shard.[^lex-tesseract-vault] What makes it remarkable is that it is
described as failing continuously — such is the power of the C'tan trapped inside that the
living metal walls of the Vault are *constantly* being
destroyed.[^lex-tesseract-vault] Repair is not maintenance here; it is the operating
condition.

Its output matches that description. An activated Tesseract Vault can age its foes to dust,
set acres ablaze, or trigger earthquakes.[^lex-tesseract-vault] Those are three quite
different categories of effect — temporal, thermal, geological — from one device, which fits
what [[I.T4.Ch14]] records about C'tan acting on physical law rather than through any
particular medium.

The Vault is therefore best understood as a weapon that consists of pointing a prisoner. The
Necrons are not channelling a power; they are opening a door a controlled amount and
standing behind it. Tesseract Vaults as war engines belong to [[VIII.T5.Ch14]] and the
Necron dynasties that field them to [[VI.T1.Ch3]].

## The Celestial Orrery {#orrery}

The last of the Necron super-weapons is not a weapon at all in appearance, and it is the
most disquieting object in this Volume.

The Celestial Orrery sits at the heart of the Necron crownworld of Thanatos, and is
considered one of the galaxy's greatest treasures.[^lex-celestial-orrery] It was crafted by
the artisans of the Oruscar Dynasty long before the War in Heaven, and is described as beyond
price in artistic value alone.[^lex-celestial-orrery]

Physically it is a web of holograms and necrodermis, in which tiny floating points of light
each represent a star in the galaxy, recorded in an intricate matrix that holds the location
of every one of them.[^lex-celestial-orrery]

As an astronomical record that would be remarkable enough — a complete catalogue of the
galaxy, compiled before the War in Heaven, by a civilisation that has since been asleep for
sixty million years. The Imperium, as [[I.T1.Ch1]] notes, cannot say how many worlds it
owns.

But it is not only a record. Manipulating the Orrery is held to act on the stars themselves:
to extinguish a light within it is to extinguish the star it
represents.[^lex-celestial-orrery] The device is simultaneously the map and the territory.

It is worth being precise about the nature of that claim, because it is exactly the kind of
assertion this codex is careful with. What the sources give is the capability and the
significance; what they do not give is any mechanism, any account of why a model built
before the War in Heaven should have causal authority over the objects it depicts, or any
statement of limits. The Orrery is described in terms of what it can do and what it is
worth, never how.

The reason it belongs in this Volume rather than among Volume IX's materiel is that it is not
really technology in the ordinary sense. Along with the Vaults and the labyrinths, it
completes a pattern: every Necron super-weapon in this Chapter operates by holding something
and letting a measured amount of it out. A shard in a cube. A god in a war engine. A galaxy
in a cabinet. The Necrons did not build weapons that create effects — they built containers,
and their entire military science is the practice of controlled release.
