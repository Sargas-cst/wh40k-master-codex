# Divine Archetypes across Pantheons

## The God-Emperor as Deity {#god-emperor}

The setting contains several kinds of god, and they are not variations on one idea. They
differ in what they are made of, in how they came to exist, and in what relationship they
have to the people who worship them. Reading them side by side is the point of this Chapter,
because each pantheon exposes something the others conceal.

The Imperial case is the strangest, and it is strange for a reason that is easy to state and
uncomfortable to sit with: the Imperium worships a god who did not want to be one, and whose
divinity is the direct consequence of a failed engineering project.

The Imperial Cult is the worship of the Emperor as Master, Defender and Father of Mankind,
and it developed *following his internment in the Golden
Throne*.[^lex-imperial-cult] The sequence matters. He was not a god who was then enthroned;
he was enthroned and then became a god. And what put him there, as [[I.T3.Ch12]] records, was
the breach in his own Webway tunnels — he sits on the Throne holding a door
closed.[^lex-webway-project]

So the object of Imperial devotion is a man immobilised by the failure of a plan whose
explicit purpose was to sever humanity from the Warp entirely and complete its evolution into
a psychic species.[^lex-webway-project] The Imperium's religion is built on the wreckage of a
project designed to make religion of that kind unnecessary.

Its content follows from its origin. The Cult is the Imperium's state religion, and in many
ways the religion *is* the state — it binds humanity together in the Emperor's
service.[^lex-imperial-cult] Its principal tenets are given as the persecution of mutants,
the abhorrence of xenos, and the worship of the Emperor and Imperial
ideals.[^lex-imperial-cult] Two of those three are prohibitions rather than devotions. The
Cult is treated in full in Volume IV Title 3, and the Emperor himself at [[VIII.T1.Ch1]].

The archetype, then: a god by administrative accident, worshipped by an institution he
founded on the premise that gods were the enemy.

## The Aeldari Pantheon {#aeldari}

The Aeldari case is the opposite. Their gods were unambiguously real, unambiguously
powerful — and were destroyed by their own worshippers' behaviour.

**Isha**, the Mother, was the goddess of harvest, fertility and nature, forming a trinity
with her daughter Lileath the Maiden and Morai-Heg the Crone.[^lex-isha] That triple
structure — maiden, mother, crone — is the most conventional mythological arrangement in the
setting, and its conventionality is worth noting: the Aeldari pantheon is the one that looks
most like a human mythology, and it is the one that has been most comprehensively wrecked.

**Kaela Mensha Khaine**, usually Khaine, the Lord of Murder, is associated with murder,
violence, strife, destruction and war.[^lex-kaela-mensha-khaine] He is one of only two gods
of the pantheon to survive the birth of Slaanesh, and the manner of his survival is the
detail that defines him: Cegorach escaped through guile, and Khaine survived through might
alone.[^lex-kaela-mensha-khaine]

But surviving is not the same as being intact. After a titanic battle in the Warp, Slaanesh
shattered Khaine into fragments, being too exhausted to destroy him
completely.[^lex-kaela-mensha-khaine] Those fragments came to rest, and from each an Avatar
of Khaine took root — walking pieces of myth, woken by the Aeldari to this day to wreak fury
in battle.[^lex-kaela-mensha-khaine] It is from Khaine that the Aspect Warriors derive their
aspects.[^lex-kaela-mensha-khaine]

The parallel with [[I.T4.Ch15]] is exact and almost certainly deliberate. A god is broken
into fragments; the fragments retain their power; the survivors keep them and deploy them as
weapons. The Necrons did this to the C'tan on purpose. Slaanesh did it to Khaine out of
exhaustion. In both cases what remains is a divine fragment used as ordnance by the people
who inherited it — the Aeldari waking an Avatar are doing, with their own war god, what the
Necrons do with a shard in a Vault.

**Cegorach**, the Laughing God, is covered at [[I.T3.Ch11]] as guardian of the Black Library,
and the survival by guile is his defining act; he gave the Aeldari irony as a defence against
hubris.[^lex-cegorach] The pantheon in full belongs to Volume VI.

The archetype: gods who were real, were defeated, and whose corpses are still in use.

## Gork and Mork {#gork-and-mork}

The Ork gods look like a joke and are, on inspection, the most theoretically interesting
entry in this Chapter.

Gork and Mork are the twin gods of the Orks, said to dwell in a region known as the Great
Green.[^lex-gork-and-mork] Their origin is given in the same terms as the Chaos powers':
Ork traits and emotions have a reflection in the Warp exactly as human and Aeldari traits do,
and it is those traits which manifest as the strong, belligerent and indestructible Ork
gods.[^lex-gork-and-mork] Compare [[I.T1.Ch1]] on how the Chaos powers formed from pooled
mortal emotion. The mechanism is identical. The Orks have gods for the same reason anyone
does.

The distinction between the two is famously slight: one is brutally cunning and the other
cunningly brutal. The Orks frequently disagree about which is which, and sometimes fight wars
over the question.[^lex-gork-and-mork] It is easy to read that as comedy, and it is comedy,
but it is also a precise statement about a species whose theology is indistinguishable from
its appetite for conflict — a doctrinal schism whose content is a distinction without a
difference, prosecuted by violence.

!!! disputed "Recorded conflict — ork-gods-precedence"

    Whether the gods preceded the Orks, or the Orks preceded the gods, is
    disputed.[^lex-gork-and-mork]

    This is a more serious question than it appears, and it bears on every pantheon in this
    Chapter. If Ork traits produce the gods, as the same article states, then the Orks must
    come first and their gods are a consequence of them. Yet the precedence is explicitly
    left open — which leaves standing the possibility of Warp gods that precede their own
    worshippers.

That unresolved question is the reason this Chapter ends here rather than with the Imperium.
Laid side by side, the four pantheons make an argument the setting never states outright.
Chaos gods are made by mortals and feed on them. Ork gods are made by mortals, possibly. The
Aeldari gods were real and were killed by what their worshippers became. And the God-Emperor
is a man who was made divine by his own failure and is worshipped by an institution founded to
deny that gods exist.

In none of the four cases does divinity come first. Every god in this Volume is downstream of
somebody's behaviour — which is the closest thing to a cosmological principle the setting
offers, and it is never said aloud.
