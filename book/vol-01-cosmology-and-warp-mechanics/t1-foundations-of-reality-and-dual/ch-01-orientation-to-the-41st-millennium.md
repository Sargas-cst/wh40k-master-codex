# Orientation to the 41st Millennium

## The Setting, and Why Its Sources Lie {#premise}

Warhammer 40,000 is a setting about a human empire that has outlived its own
competence. The Imperium of Man spans, it is commonly said, a million
worlds.[^lex-imperium-of-man] That figure is worth pausing on, because of how the
sources hold it. The same article reporting it also records that the Adeptus
Administratum — the bureaucracy whose entire function is to count things — estimates
about a million worlds and does not know the true number.[^lex-imperium-of-man] The
Imperium's most-quoted statistic about itself is an approximation its own civil
service cannot confirm. The same article offers a third formulation alongside the other
two — that the Imperium controls *countless* worlds[^lex-imperium-of-man] — and the three
do not agree on whether the number is knowable at all, let alone what it is.

That pattern is not incidental, and a reference work has to decide early how to handle
it. Much of what is written about this setting is written from inside it, by narrators
with reasons to be wrong: propagandists, censors, and functionaries recording what is
safe to record. Where this codex reports Imperial doctrine, it is reporting what the
Imperium asserts and enforces, which is frequently not what is true. Where the two
come apart, the text says so.

The setting's authors compound this deliberately. Consider the date. Almost everything
written about the setting places events in the 41st and 42nd Millennia — yet
Lexicanum's article on Imperial dating attaches a conflicting-sources marker to its own
first use of "42nd millennium", noting that whether the period following the opening of
the Great Rift is actually M42 has been *intentionally* put into doubt by Games
Workshop.[^lex-imperial-dating-system] The setting declines to say what year it is, and
that refusal is [[d:is-it-really-m42|recorded here as a disputed fact]] in its own
right. Not carelessness, but a choice.

This has a consequence worth holding onto: contradiction here is not always a mistake
to be corrected. Sometimes it is the content. A codex that resolved every conflict into
a single tidy account would be a *less* accurate description of Warhammer 40,000 than
one that reports the conflicts, because unreliability is a designed property of the
material rather than a defect in it. The
[[a:disputed-facts]] exists for that reason.

"Grimdark" is the term most often used for the setting's register, but it is not Games
Workshop's own word for it — it arose outside the studio and stuck. It is used here as
shorthand for a tone, and nothing more is claimed for it.

## The Enemies the Imperium Names {#enemies}

The Imperium organises the galaxy by what it must fight. Its doctrine names three
kinds of enemy, and the categories are less symmetrical than they first appear.

The first is Chaos — not an empire but a condition. Chaos is described as part of the
psychic energy composing [[s:the-warp]], effectively inseparable from it: a limitless
ocean of spiritual and emotional energy that is at once a raw force of change and a
corrupting one.[^lex-chaos] Its powers formed from the pooled emotions of mortal
beings — anguish, desire, hatred, pride — accumulating in the psychic mirror of the
material universe until they gained sentience.[^lex-chaos] This is a structurally
strange enemy: it is generated by the beings it destroys. It is also named differently
depending on who is speaking — the Eldar call it the Primordial Annihilator, and Lorgar
and the Word Bearers the Primordial Truth[^lex-chaos] — which is a reminder that even the
categories in this Section are Imperial ones rather than neutral descriptions. Volume V
treats Chaos and its powers in full, beginning at [[V.T1.Ch1]].

The second is the xenos. The word is simply the Imperial term for "alien", covering all
non-human sentient species.[^lex-xenos] Imperial doctrine holds humanity the rightful
ruler of the whole galaxy, and part of the Imperial Creed is that any alien judged to
threaten mankind's survival must be contained or destroyed.[^lex-xenos] What that
judgement amounts to in practice is recorded plainly: nearly all xenos are feared and
hated by most Imperials, to the point that a xenos is at a measurable disadvantage in
any social or diplomatic exchange with them.[^lex-xenos] Nor is the category closed: Imperial Explorators discover
new alien races every year, most of which are ignored, contained, or eradicated where
they are judged a threat.[^lex-xenos] The species themselves are the subject of Volume VI,
starting at [[VI.T1.Ch1]].

The third category complicates the scheme. Aliens are grouped, in Imperial thinking,
alongside mutants, heretics and deviants — all of them enemies of mankind, all threats
to the survival of the species.[^lex-xenos] The principal tenets of the Imperial Creed
are given as the persecution of mutants, the abhorrence of xenos, and the worship of
the Emperor and Imperial ideals.[^lex-imperial-cult] So the third enemy is internal,
and it is defined by deviation rather than by hostility. A human born wrong belongs to
the same doctrinal category as an invading species.

It is worth being precise about what this does and does not describe. These are not
three balanced factions in opposition. They are three headings under which the Imperium
files everything it intends to destroy — and one of those headings points inward.

## Tech Decay and Religious Dogma {#decay-and-dogma}

The Imperium does not understand the technology it runs on. This is the setting's
central irony, and it has a documented history.

During the Dark Age of Technology, humanity possessed Standard Template Construct
systems: artificial intelligences said to have contained the entirety of human
technological knowledge to that point.[^lex-standard-template-construct] After that age
ended, the systems became progressively rarer until they were lost altogether. What
survives into the present does so only because it was preserved in STC *hard copies* —
degraded transcriptions of a machine nobody can now
rebuild.[^lex-standard-template-construct]

What ended that age is recorded, though not consistently. The revolt of the Men of Iron
devastated the human race and heralded its close.[^lex-age-of-strife] The rise of
psykers on human worlds followed, compounded by the eruption of massive and persistent
Warp storms; travel through the Warp grew progressively more dangerous until it was all
but impossible, isolating colony worlds for millennia.[^lex-age-of-strife] Worlds
dependent on imported food starved. The period is treated in full at [[III.T2.Ch9]].

The isolation lasted long enough to change the species. Over those millennia new kinds of
human evolved to suit the worlds they were stranded on, becoming the abhuman strains — the
Ratlings, the Ogryns, the Squats — while mankind's technological achievements were lost
and many worlds regressed to outright barbarity.[^lex-age-of-strife] The Imperium that
emerged afterwards was assembled from those fragments, which is part of why it is so
uneven a thing: it did not build a civilisation so much as reconquer the wreckage of one.

!!! disputed "Recorded conflict — age-of-strife-start-date"

    Two editions give two answers, two millennia apart: **M23** and
    **M25**.[^lex-age-of-strife] Each is stated as fact in its own source, and nothing
    reconciles them.

The consequence for the present is that the Imperium's relationship with machinery
became religious rather than technical. Understanding was replaced by observance. The
Imperial Cult is the state religion, and the source is blunt that in many ways the
religion *is* the state, binding humanity together in the Emperor's
service.[^lex-imperial-cult] Heresy against it is punished
severely.[^lex-imperial-cult] The organisation that maintains the Imperium's machines
is treated at [[IV.T6.Ch19]], and the Cult itself in Volume IV Title 3.

The point to carry forward is that decay and dogma are one phenomenon rather than two.
A civilisation that has lost the ability to explain its own tools will preserve them by
ritual — and will eventually mistake the ritual for the explanation.
