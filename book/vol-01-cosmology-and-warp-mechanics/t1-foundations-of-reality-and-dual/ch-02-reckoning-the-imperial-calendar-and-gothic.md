# Reckoning: the Imperial Calendar & Gothic Tongue

## Millennial Dating and Check Numbers {#dating-and-check-numbers}

The Imperial Calendar was created by the Emperor sometime before the end of the Great
Crusade, and is also recorded as the Terran Standard
Calendar.[^lex-imperial-dating-system] By the 42nd Millennium at least five
"non-heretical" variations of the original were in use.[^lex-imperial-dating-system] It
bases its years on the Anno Domini system, beginning at AD
1.[^lex-imperial-dating-system]

Most citizens use only the year, written **456.M41** — or, with the year alone,
**456/M41**. These specify the year on Terra; most systems keep their own local
calendars for local events.[^lex-imperial-dating-system] For administrative purposes the
date expands into four parts:[^lex-imperial-dating-system]

| Part | Example | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Check number | `0` | How reliably the date relates to time on Terra |
| Year fraction | `123` | Position within the year, in thousandths |
| Year | `456` | Year within the millennium |
| Millennium | `M41` | Millennium, counted from the first AD |

Written in full: **0.123.456.M41**, or **0123456.M41**.[^lex-imperial-dating-system]

The [[g:check-number]] deserves attention, because it measures not precision but
*provenance*. It runs 0 to 9, and each value records how the information reached Terra.
Class 0 means the event occurred on Terra itself; class 1, within the Sol System. Class
2 means the source was in direct psychic contact with Terra or Sol; class 3, in contact
with a class 2 source; class 4 with a class 3 — a documented chain of hearsay. Classes
6, 7 and 8 mean the source had no psychic contact with any class 0–5 source at all, and
can be tied to a dated sequence only within one year, ten years, or more than ten years
respectively. Class 9 means the date was approximated from a non-Imperial source
entirely.[^lex-imperial-dating-system]

Why such a scale is necessary is stated directly: check numbers are vital because of the
temporal distortions the Warp can inflict on real space.[^lex-imperial-dating-system] An
empire dependent on [[s:warp-travel]] cannot assume two observers agree on when anything
happened, so it built the disagreement into its notation. The Imperium's dating system
is, read plainly, an admission encoded as a standard.

The [[g:year-fraction]] divides each year into 1,000 equal
parts.[^lex-imperial-dating-system] How that division is performed is not recorded
anywhere.

!!! undefined "No source defines this — imperial-year-fraction"

    Nothing in the setting states how the year is divided into a thousand parts. Figures
    for the length of a single fraction do circulate — around eight and three-quarter
    hours — but they are arithmetic performed by readers, not anything the Imperium or its
    authors ever specified. **No duration is given here**, because there is none to give.

!!! disputed "Recorded conflict — millennium-year-range"

    How the years *inside* a millennium are numbered is disputed, and the notation
    needs unpacking before the disagreement makes sense.

    The majority reading, carried by three citations, runs **001 to 000**. That is
    not a countdown and time is not running backwards: the field holds three
    digits, so the thousandth and final year of a millennium overflows to `000`.
    The article's own worked example confirms it — the year 1998 is written
    `998.M2`, which places M2 across the years 1001 to 2000, making the year 2000
    `000.M2`.[^lex-imperial-dating-system] The count runs forward throughout; only
    the notation wraps.

    The 5th Edition Rulebook instead runs **000 to 999**, starting at
    zero.[^lex-imperial-dating-system] Lexicanum flags the disagreement itself and
    notes that every source claims to follow the Anno Domini system — which has no
    year zero, and therefore runs 001 to 000. That is an internal-consistency
    argument for the majority reading rather than a resolution, and this codex
    records both.

The system has also failed in-universe. Between the Great Crusade and the formation of
the Great Rift, a conflict arose within the Ordo Chronos over what the correct date
actually was, with five competing factional interpretations differing by roughly a
thousand years and further heretical positions beyond
them.[^lex-imperial-dating-system] It is called the Chronostrife. An institution founded
to keep time lost track of it by a millennium, then split into sects over the question.

## High Gothic and Low Gothic {#high-and-low-gothic}

The Imperium speaks two related languages, and the distance between them measures how
long it has been decaying.

[[g:high-gothic]] — also recorded as Archaic, Principle and Prime Gothic — is the
hierarchic tongue: the language of ancient institutional titles, regarded as
holy.[^lex-high-gothic] It is the Imperium's official language, used by officials,
nobility, the Ecclesiarchy and in high-level negotiation, and is preferred where clarity
and absolute certainty are required.[^lex-high-gothic]

Its origin is mundane. High Gothic developed during the Dark Age of Technology out of
the common tongue of the period — an assimilation of English, European and Pacific
languages — and arose in the Merican/Pacific region of Terra. It was the universal
medium of written record until the Age of Strife, spoken as a first language by many and
as a second by almost everyone.[^lex-high-gothic] Its idioms and vocabulary now appear
archaic and mystic, and many of its words have acquired religious significance across
the intervening millennia.[^lex-high-gothic] It has remained essentially unchanged
since.[^lex-high-gothic]

That last point is the telling one. High Gothic is holy because it is *old*, and because
the technical manuals and ancient works recorded in it are no longer understood — the
same mechanism [[I.T1.Ch1]] describes for machinery, operating on language instead.
Nothing about the tongue was sacred while it was current. Reverence accumulated as
comprehension drained away.

[[g:low-gothic]], or Imperial Gothic, is the common language of the present Imperium: a
bastardised descendant of High Gothic combining elements of several oriental languages of
ancient Terra, changed so greatly over the millennia that it now bears almost no
resemblance to the tongues it came from.[^lex-low-gothic] It is spoken as a first
language on almost all civilised worlds and accepted as a second throughout Imperial
space, excepting some feudal and feral worlds.[^lex-low-gothic]

It is not, however, one language. Low Gothic varies from planet to planet and even
region to region, so that two Imperial citizens from different worlds may struggle to
communicate at all.[^lex-low-gothic] Isolated, medieval and feral worlds may carry
several indigenous tongues descended either from High Gothic or from ancient Terran
languages. Some of that fragmentation was deliberate: during the Dark Age of Technology
it was common for worlds to be settled by small, self-financed communities of
isolationists, often ethnic minorities attempting to reconstitute a national identity
away from an overpopulated Earth — and some set out to revive dead or moribund languages
as a source of communal strength.[^lex-low-gothic]

Written Low Gothic uses capitalisation, and there is evidence it retains letters from
ancient Terran alphabets, recognised as script rather than as meaningless marks by at
least one M41 observer.[^lex-low-gothic]

A word on forms of address. Individual Imperial titles are everywhere — Magos, Canoness,
Lord Inquisitor — but nothing describes a unified etiquette governing them. There is no
Imperial protocol, only a great many separate customs belonging to separate institutions,
each treated with its own body in Volume IV.
