# Anatomy of the Soul

## What a Soul Is {#what-a-soul-is}

Every creature of flesh and blood in the material universe — with some noted
exceptions — also has an existence in the Warp. That second existence is the soul: a
shadow-self formed of pure energy.[^lex-soul] It is not, in this setting, a purely
religious proposition. It is the mechanism on which everything else in this Title
depends.

Two claims follow from it, and both are load-bearing. The first is that the soul is
where certain ordinary human faculties come from — resolve, vigour, determination are
described as drawn from it.[^lex-soul] The second is that it is the aperture through
which psychic power reaches realspace: a [[g:psyker]] draws the energy used to cast
psychic powers from the soul.[^lex-soul] Psionics, the general term for the use of such
powers, is defined as energy drawn from the Warp *through* the soul of the
practitioner.[^lex-psionics] The soul is not a battery. It is a hole in the wall.

That framing explains why the setting treats psykers as a security problem rather than
a resource to be cultivated, a point [[I.T2.Ch9]] takes up. If the soul is an opening,
then every psyker is an opening, and openings admit traffic in both directions.

Souls are sometimes spoken of as though they had brightness. The souls of the most
powerful psykers are said to burn like supernovae in the Empyrean, drawing daemons the way
a fire draws moths.[^lex-the-assignment] That is a figure of speech, and the setting marks
it as one — there is no scale of soul-brightness, and nothing measures it.

Precision about souls is scarce in general. What the setting states about them is thin, and
this Chapter is short accordingly; the brevity belongs to the subject.

## Species and Their Warp Presence {#species}

The soul is not distributed evenly across the galaxy's species, and the variation is
recorded plainly enough to be listed.

Psykers occur in many species: human psykers, Aeldari warlocks and Ork weirdboyz are all
given as examples of the same underlying phenomenon.[^lex-the-assignment] At the other
extreme sit beings composed of nothing else — daemons of the Warp are described as made
of purely psychic energies.[^lex-the-assignment] The setting therefore runs a spectrum
rather than a binary: matter at one end, pure Warp-stuff at the other, and most sentient
life somewhere between.

Two positions on that spectrum matter more than the rest.

The **T'au** are recorded as a species devoid of psykers and psychic
powers.[^lex-the-assignment] They are described as psychic blunts with virtually no
presence in the Warp, and they never develop psychic powers at all.[^lex-soul] This is
not a cultural prohibition but an apparent property of the species. Volume VI Title 5
covers the T'au proper; what belongs here is that the setting contains at least one
major civilisation for which the entire apparatus of this Title simply does not apply.

At the far end are the **anti-psykers**: humans who are not merely unpsychic but
negatively so. The Assignment article calls them blanks, and pairs them with Necron
pariahs as beings that could be called anti-psychic.[^lex-the-assignment] They are the
subject of [[I.T2.Ch6]], because the Imperium classifies them on the same scale it uses
for psykers — at the opposite end of it.

The average human sits nowhere interesting. A typical Imperial citizen is rated at a
level that cannot draw on the Immaterium consciously or unconsciously, and cannot be
possessed without outside psychic influence.[^lex-the-assignment] The vast majority of
mankind poses no psychic security threat whatsoever. Everything the Imperium builds to
police psykers is built for a small minority — which is worth remembering when reading
[[I.T2.Ch9]] on the scale of the machinery.

## The Soul at Death {#at-death}

The soul is parted from the body at death.[^lex-soul] What happens next is one of the
bleakest propositions in the setting, and the sources are unusually direct about it.

The soul of a dead mortal is cast adrift in the limitless sea of energy that is the
Warp, and eventually the souls of all but the strongest are drawn to the Chaos gods to
feed their appetites.[^lex-soul] The Warp is the final destination of the deceased souls
of most species in the galaxy, where they swirl in its eddies and many are consumed by
preying daemons.[^lex-warp] For the great majority — particularly the minimally psychic
— there is a mercy of sorts: they swiftly lose any sentience and perceive only the
nothingness of oblivion.[^lex-warp] Oblivion is the *good* outcome.

The moment of separation appears to be tied to the death of the brain. There is evidence
that the parting occurs when the brain dies or is destroyed, at least for creatures that
have one.[^lex-soul] The C'tan Zarhulash is quoted describing a human head as an
"endoskeletal box housing the organ of material-immaterial
interface",[^lex-soul] and Belisarius Cawl — a magos with knowledge of psychic
technology — treats preservation of the head as bearing on the preservation of the
soul.[^lex-soul] Most other organs can be lost and replaced with bionics without killing
the person; the heart included.[^lex-soul]

The word the setting reaches for is *interface*, not anchor — a point where two realities
meet inside a skull, rather than a tether holding one to the other. The difference matters,
because an anchor implies something that could be cut, and no such mechanism is described.

The religious consequence is that the various species' funerary technologies are not
superstition but engineering against a documented threat. Aeldari spirit stones, the
Emperor's claimed protection of the virtuous, the Orks' Great Green — all are attempts to
keep a soul out of the general circulation described above.[^lex-warp] Those are treated
where they belong: the Aeldari in Volume VI, the Imperial claim in [[VIII.T1.Ch1]].
